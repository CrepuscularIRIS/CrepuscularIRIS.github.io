# Common Corpus: A 2T-Token Ethical Pre-Training Dataset That Bypasses Copyright Altogether

**Original title**: Common Corpus: The Largest Collection of Ethical Data for LLM Pre-Training  
**Authors**: Langlais et al. | **Published**: OpenReview, 2026  
**Link**: [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=0wSlFpMsGb)

---

## One-Line Summary

Common Corpus drops **~2 trillion tokens** of openly licensed text — books, government archives, scientific papers, code — into the open, giving the research community a copyright-free drop-in replacement for the proprietary corpora that currently power frontier LLM training.

---

## Why This Is Worth Your Time

Two things make this paper stand out.

First, the copyright problem is not theoretical. Several high-profile lawsuits (Google, OpenAI, Anthropic all named as defendants) have made it clear that training on scraped web text carries serious legal tail risk. A dataset this size that demonstrably sidesteps that risk is not a nice-to-have — it's infrastructure the entire open-source AI ecosystem has been waiting for.

Second, the geographic diversity angle is underappreciated. Most large pre-training corpora skew heavily English because English content dominates the open web. Common Corpus explicitly includes **low-resource European languages** that almost never appear in The Pile, C4, or RefinedWeb. That changes who can build capable models in those language communities.

---

## Core Method (3-Minute Version)

The authors take the opposite approach of general web crawlers. Instead of grabbing everything and then litigating later, they start from a curated list of sources known to be legally clean:

- Government publications (US federal, EU, open data portals)
- Academic papers released under permissive open-access licenses
- Open-source code repositories
- Public-domain books and out-of-copyright works
- Wikimedia and similar clearly-licensed content

They then apply standard deduplication and quality filtering — nothing exotic in the pipeline itself. The key contribution is the **provenance discipline**: every token can be traced to a source with a known, permissive license. This isn't just "we tried our best" provenance — it's a dataset built from the ground up to be auditable.

The structure mirrors The Pile or C4: domain-weighted sections where code, academic text, and books each contribute proportional slices of the total token count.

One honest caveat: at web-scale, no one fully documents every filtering decision. The paper is transparent about its intent but doesn't provide a complete audit trail of every data decision — which is a limitation worth noting.

## Key Results

| Dimension | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Total tokens** | ~2 trillion |
| **Languages** | High-resource European + low-resource languages rarely seen in mainstream corpora |
| **Code** | Large volume with diversity across domains and time periods |
| **License** | Fully open / permissively licensed — no copyright risk |

No concrete benchmark numbers (e.g., perplexity vs. The Pile) are provided in the source. The paper presents this as a dataset release; downstream evaluation by the community will determine its competitive standing.

---

## Limits and Unanswered Questions

- **No published benchmarks.** It's unclear how models trained on Common Corpus compare to those trained on The Pile, C4, or RedPajama on standard benchmarks. The paper releases data, not a trained model.
- **Low-resource coverage depth.** The paper claims inclusion of low-resource languages, but the actual token volume per language is not specified. "Included" and "included meaningfully" are different things.
- **Reproducibility of the pipeline.** The data collection pipeline details (exact deduplication thresholds, quality heuristics) are described at a high level but not fully reproducible from the paper alone.
- **Temporal coverage.** Code and academic data have natural recency cutoffs based on licensing windows. The corpus's coverage of post-2024 knowledge is unclear.
