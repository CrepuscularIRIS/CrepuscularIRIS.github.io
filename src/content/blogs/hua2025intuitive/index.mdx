# Intuitive Fine-Tuning: Collapsing RLHF into One Training Step

**Original title:** *Intuitive Fine-Tuning: Towards Simplifying Alignment into a Single Process*  
**Authors:** Hua et al. | **Published at:** ACL 2025  
**Link:** [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.6/)

---

## One-Line Summary

This paper proposes replacing the fragmented RLHF pipeline — supervised fine-tuning, reward modeling, and PPO-style optimization — with a single end-to-end alignment process that trains in one intuitive step.

---

## Why This Is Worth Your Time

**The engineering value is immediate.** Anyone who has shipped an aligned model knows the RLHF pipeline is a maintenance nightmare: three separate stages, brittle hyperparameters, and a reward model that drifts silently. If Hua et al. can genuinely collapse this into one process, the operational cost of alignment drops dramatically.

**It also signals a broader shift.** The field is moving away from "stack more stages" toward "find the right objective." DPO and KTO already showed you can skip PPO; this paper asks whether you can skip the *entire* pipeline architecture. That question matters even if the answer is only partially yes.

---

## Core Method (3-Minute Version)

The standard RLHF pipeline looks like an assembly line: first SFT shapes the model's language, then a reward model learns human preferences, then PPO or a variant optimizes against that reward. Each stage has its own data, its own hyperparameters, and its own failure modes.

Hua et al. ask a deceptively simple question: what if alignment is just another fine-tuning objective, not a separate optimization problem?

The intuition they pursue is that preference signals can be incorporated directly into the training loss without first distilling them into a reward function. Instead of teaching a model to *predict* rewards and then *optimize* them, you teach it to move toward preferred outputs and away from rejected ones in the same gradient step. This collapses the architecture from three models and two optimizers down to one model and one optimizer.

The practical appeal is obvious: fewer moving parts, less hyperparameter search, and no reward model to maintain. The theoretical question is whether this simplification preserves alignment quality or sacrifices it for convenience.

## Key Results

No concrete numbers were available in the source material at the time of drafting. The paper's primary contribution is architectural — a proof that single-process alignment is feasible — rather than a benchmark sweep.

---

## Limits and Unanswered Questions

**Empirical coverage is thin in the source.** Without access to the full paper's tables, it is unclear whether the single-process approach matches or exceeds DPO/KTO on standard preference benchmarks like MT-Bench or Arena Elo.

**Theoretical guarantees are unverified here.** Simplifying the pipeline is attractive, but if the unified objective introduces new failure modes — for example, mode collapse or sensitivity to preference noise — those trade-offs need explicit characterization.

**Scalability to large models is an open question.** Methods that work cleanly at 7B parameters often encounter hidden instabilities at 70B+. The paper's ACL venue suggests NLP-scale experiments; LLM-scale validation would strengthen the claim significantly.

## Further Reading

- **DPO** (Rafailov et al., 2023): The foundational work showing PPO can be eliminated entirely by optimizing directly on preference data.
- **KTO** (Ethayarajh et al., 2024): Extends the "no-reward-model" direction by using binary feedback instead of pairwise preferences.
- **RSO** (Liu et al., 2023): Another attempt to simplify alignment through rejection sampling, worth comparing to the single-process approach here.
