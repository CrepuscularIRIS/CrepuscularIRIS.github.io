# Jet Expansions: Cutting Through the Black Box of Language Models

**Original Title**: Decomposing LLM Computation with Jets  
**Authors**: Chen et al.  
**Published**: OpenReview | 2026  
**Link**: [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=u6JLh0BO5h)

---

## One-Line Summary

Jet Expansions gives mechanists a formal mathematical knife to carve through LLM computation graphs — revealing explicit input-to-output paths that were previously buried in tangled residual streams, and rigorously justifying why the popular Logit Lens technique actually works.

---

## Why This Is Worth Your Time

If you've ever wondered *how* an LLM gets from "the" to " cat" inside a 32-layer transformer, you've hit the wall that confronts every mechanistic interpretability researcher. The field has two rough tribes: those who use heuristics like Logit Lens to peek at intermediate activations, and those who demand rigorous causal analysis. They don't always speak the same language.

Jet Expansions bridges that gap — not with another clever hack, but with a **formal mathematical framework**. The paper takes jet operators (a generalization of truncated Taylor series from differential geometry) and applies them to the computational graph of a transformer. The result: a principled decomposition of what the model is doing, and a theorem showing that Logit Lens is a special case of this richer structure.

This matters because it gives the field a **shared vocabulary and a proof system**. When you say "this path is doing X," you're now working inside a framework where "what X means" can be formalized.

---

## Core Method (3-Minute Version)

Think of an LLM as a deeply nested function. Information doesn't teleport from input to output — it flows through residual streams, attention heads, and MLP layers across **L** layers. The problem: these flows are entangled. By the time you reach layer 20, you can't point at a single computation and say "this is responsible for predicting *cat*."

Jet Expansions solves this by analogy. Just as a Taylor series breaks a smooth function into a polynomial plus a remainder, a **jet expansion** breaks the LLM's computation into:

- **Explicit input-to-output paths** — discrete computational chains that contribute directly to the output
- **A remainder term** — everything else that doesn't factor neatly into paths

The **jet operator** is the knife. It truncates the Taylor-like expansion at a chosen depth, cleanly separating what can be decomposed into paths from what can't. Each residual update in a transformer becomes a coordinate in this expansion, and the structure of the paths falls out naturally.

One striking result: the number of distinct paths grows **super-exponentially** with recursive residual depth. This isn't a bug — it explains why mechanistic interpretability gets harder as models get deeper, and why dominant paths may dominate by a wide margin.

## Key Results

| Aspect | Finding |
|--------|---------|
| Framework | Jet Expansions — a rigorous decomposition framework using jet operators |
| Scope | Systematically decomposes models into explicit paths + remainders |
| Path growth | (Super-)exponential with respect to recursive residual depth |
| Prior work | Rigorously grounds and subsumes Logit Lens |

The table is thin because the paper's contributions are **theoretical** rather than empirical. No benchmark tables here — the contribution is the framework itself, not a new SOTA number.

---

## Limits and Unanswered Questions

The paper doesn't provide concrete numbers or experiments on real models. "Super-exponential path growth" is a structural result, but:

- How many dominant paths actually dominate in practice? (Likely few — but unmeasured)
- Does this framework make verified predictions about circuit-level behavior?
- Can the remainder term be further analyzed, or does it stay opaque?

Also: the framework assumes differentiable models. Discrete operations (sampling, top-k) break the jet structure — which limits applicability during inference.
