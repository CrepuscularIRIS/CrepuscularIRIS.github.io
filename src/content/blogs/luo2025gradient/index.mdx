# 📄 Gradient Decomposition and Alignment for Incremental Object Detection

**Original title:** Gradient Decomposition and Alignment for Incremental Object Detection  
**Authors:** Wenlong Luo et al. | **Venue:** ICCV 2025  
**Link:** [CVF Open Access](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/html/Luo_Gradient_Decomposition_and_Alignment_for_Incremental_Object_Detection_ICCV_2025_paper.html)

---

## One-Line Summary

Instead of tweaking outputs or freezing features, this paper **operates directly on gradients** — decomposing them by class and aligning old-class gradients against new-class ones — to stop incremental object detectors from forgetting what they already knew.

---

## Why This Is Worth Your Time

Incremental object detection is one of those problems that sounds niche until you realize every real-world vision system faces it: a warehouse robot trained on 50 SKUs suddenly needs to recognize 50 more, without re-labeling the entire historical dataset. The standard playbook — replay buffers, feature distillation, parameter freezing — treats the symptom (output drift) rather than the cause (gradient interference).

This paper's bet is that **the interference happens in gradient space, so the fix should live there too.** That is a cleaner abstraction than most prior work. If the decomposition holds up empirically, it becomes a reusable primitive for any continual-learning detector, not just a one-off architectural tweak.

---

## Core Method (3-Minute Version)

### The problem in one picture

When you fine-tune a detector on new classes, the gradients that update the shared backbone do not "know" which classes they serve. A gradient step that improves detection for "new-class A" can silently degrade "old-class B" because both classes share the same weights. That is catastrophic forgetting at the gradient level.

### The proposed fix

The authors decompose the overall gradient **g** into class-specific components — roughly, projecting the full gradient onto directions that matter for each class. Once decomposed, they **align** the old-class gradient components with the new-class ones, ensuring that a step forward for new classes does not become a step backward for old classes.

Think of it as negotiating a traffic intersection: instead of letting every car (class) enter the intersection (weight update) at once and crash, the method separates the traffic streams and synchronizes their green lights.

### Why this is finer-grained than prior art

- **Output-level methods** (e.g., distillation) only notice forgetting after the model has already produced wrong logits.
- **Feature-level methods** (e.g., frozen backbones) stop learning in entire sub-networks, which hurts plasticity.
- **Gradient-level alignment** intervenes at the exact moment knowledge would be overwritten, without sacrificing the model's ability to learn.

The catch: gradient decomposition is not free. It requires either extra forward passes or careful approximation to avoid turning every training step into a compute bottleneck. The paper presumably addresses this, but the source note does not include architectural details.

## Limits and Unanswered Questions

- **Scalability to many classes.** Decomposing and aligning gradients for 100+ classes could turn the training loop into an O(N²) affair. The paper likely proposes approximations, but the exact cost curve is unknown without the full text.
- **Dependence on gradient linearity.** The method assumes that class-specific gradients can be cleanly separated. In deep networks with heavy feature reuse, this assumption may break — especially in the later layers of a detector where class semantics are highly entangled.
- **No code or preprint link yet.** At curation time, only the CVF HTML landing page was available. Reproducibility will depend on whether the authors release code alongside the camera-ready.

## Further Reading

- **iCaRL** (Rebuffi et al., CVPR 2017) — the classic rehearsal baseline that most incremental-classification work still compares against. The object-detection analogue is less settled, making this paper's contribution timely.
- **EWC / Elastic Weight Consolidation** (Kirkpatrick et al., PNAS 2017) — another gradient-aware continual-learning method, but at the parameter level rather than the gradient-decomposition level. Comparing the two would clarify whether decomposition beats consolidation in detection settings.
