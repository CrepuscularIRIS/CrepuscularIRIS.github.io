## One-Line Summary

Two on-device small language models — a 2.6B-parameter Gemma 4 E2B and a 600M-parameter Qwen3 0.6B — entered a production Android word game and, after five days and 204 commits, the team replaced most of the LLM logic with curated word lists and deterministic rules.

## Why This Is Worth Your Time

Every vendor demo shows an SLM running smoothly on a phone. Almost no one publishes what happens in the weeks after the demo, when real users, real hardware variance, and real structured generation requirements meet the model. This paper is a practitioner unfiltered — a small development team building Palabrita, a word-guessing game, documenting each failure mode as they discovered it.

This is the anti-narrative to the "local model, no cloud, privacy-safe" sales pitch. The authors don't argue that on-device SLMs are bad; they show that **the gap between "runs in demo" and "production-reliable" is enormous**, and that closing it requires architectural humbling, not better prompts.

For anyone actually shipping AI features in mobile apps, this is more instructive than ten benchmark papers.

## Core Method (3-Minute Version)

The team started from what you'd expect: the LLM generates everything. Full structured puzzles — the word, its category, its difficulty rating, five hint sentences — all produced by the model at runtime in JSON format.

Within hours of the 5-day sprint, this broke. The problems were specific:

- **Structured generation hallucination**: Models would drop fields, invent categories, or produce malformed JSON under battery/thermal pressure.
- **Latency variance**: On-device inference on a mid-range Android device was fast enough in isolation but became unpredictable under OS scheduling, other apps, and thermal throttling.
- **Prompt sensitivity**: Small changes in the generation request produced wildly different outputs — fine in a chat interface, catastrophic for deterministic game logic that expects a specific schema.

The iterative fix was an **architecture pivot in stages**:

1. Strip the LLM from generating puzzle words entirely → human-curated word lists.
2. Keep the LLM for creative hint generation, but constrain output to just three short sentences.
3. Add a deterministic fallback: if generation exceeds a timeout or produces invalid output, serve a pre-written hint from the curated set.

The final architecture is a hybrid: humans own the hard, high-stakes decisions (which words, which categories, which difficulty tiers); the LLM only handles low-stakes creative text generation where occasional weirdness is tolerable.

## Key Results

The paper is qualitative — no quantitative failure rates, latency numbers, or battery drain figures are reported. The results are architectural:

- **Radical architecture pivot**: from LLM generating full structured puzzles (JSON with word, category, difficulty, five hints) to curated word lists + LLM generating only three short hints with deterministic fallback.
- **Five categories of failures** specific to on-device SLM integration identified through a 5-day, 204-commit development sprint.
- **Empirical evidence** that even 2.6B-parameter Gemma 4 E2B and 600M-parameter Qwen3 0.6B models struggle with structured generation reliability in production mobile contexts.
- No concrete numbers (latency, failure rate, battery impact) are provided — qualitative practitioner observations only.

## Limits and Unanswered Questions

The study is honest about its scope:

- **Single-app case study**: Palabrita is a word-guessing game. Whether these failure patterns generalize to, say, a note-taking app or a camera feature is genuinely unknown.
- **5-day sprint**: The observations are concentrated and dense, but there's no longitudinal view. Do the failure patterns change after months of real-user traffic?
- **No quantitative metrics**: The paper documents what broke but not how often, how slowly, or how much battery was consumed. This makes it hard to compare with alternatives.
- **Qualitative only**: Without benchmarks or A/B comparisons, it's impossible to know whether the deterministic fallback is "good enough" or just "less visibly broken."
