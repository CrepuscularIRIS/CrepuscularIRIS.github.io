# 📄 The Impossibility of Fair LLMs

**Original title:** The Impossibility of Fair LLMs  
**Authors:** Jacy Reese Anthis, Kristian Lum, Michael Ekstrand, Avi Feller, Chenhao Tan  
**Published at:** ACL 2025 (Long Paper)  
**Date:** 2025  
**Link:** [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.5/)

---

## One-Line Summary

No single LLM can be fair to everyone — the paper proves that competing fairness definitions are structurally incompatible in generative settings, so the real task is not "fixing bias" but negotiating trade-offs.

---

## Why This Is Worth Your Time

Most fairness work treats bias as a bug to be patched. Anthis et al. treat it as a **mathematical axiom** — something baked into the problem itself. This reframing has two immediate consequences.

**First, it kills the fantasy of a universally fair model.** Vendors and regulators often speak as if "unbiased AI" is an engineering target. The paper shows it is not. Any LLM that serves multiple stakeholder groups with conflicting values will necessarily fail at least one widely accepted fairness criterion. This is not a limitation of current training methods; it is a limitation of the formal definitions themselves.

**Second, it forces the conversation toward governance, not optimization.** If you cannot satisfy all fairness criteria simultaneously, the question becomes *which* criteria to privilege and *who* gets to decide. That is a political question dressed in statistical clothing, and the paper makes the dress code explicit.

---

## Core Method (3-Minute Version)

The argument builds on a lineage of impossibility results that stretches back to Kleinberg et al. (2016) and Chouldechova (2017) in binary classification. Those papers proved that **demographic parity**, **equalized odds**, and **calibration** cannot all hold at the same time unless the base rates are identical across groups. Anthis et al. extend this trilemma to large language models, where the output space is not a single label but open-ended text.

In the generative setting, "fairness" fragments even further. An LLM may be asked to summarize news, generate advice, or role-play a dialogue partner. Each task implicates different stakeholders, different harms, and different notions of what "neutral" means. The authors formalize this multiplicity and prove that **no generative model can simultaneously satisfy a minimal set of fairness axioms** when stakeholders hold incompatible values.

The proof strategy is conceptually close to Arrow's impossibility theorem in social-choice theory: define a set of desirable properties, show that they logically conflict, and conclude that the only resolution is to drop at least one axiom or to accept that the choice mechanism must be contextual. The paper's framing is therefore not "here is a better fairness metric" but rather **"here is why the search for a single best metric is doomed."**

---

## Key Results

- **No concrete numbers in source.** The paper is theorem-driven; its contribution is a proof structure rather than empirical benchmarks.
- The core result is an **impossibility theorem**: given mild assumptions about stakeholder diversity, any LLM fails at least one of demographic parity, equalized odds, or calibration-like analogues in the text-generation setting.
- The authors also show that **naive ensembling** — training separate "fair" adapters for each group — does not escape the impossibility; it merely shifts the violation to a different axiom.

---

## Limits and Unanswered Questions

The paper's formalism is powerful but leaves operational gaps.

**Who decides the trade-off?** The theorem tells us a choice must be made, but it does not tell us *how* to make it democratically. The governance mechanism is outside the paper's scope, yet it is the only thing that matters once the impossibility is accepted.

**How tight are the assumptions?** The proof relies on stakeholders having "incompatible values." In practice, many conflicts are partial or contextual. A tighter characterization of when the impossibility bites — versus when approximate coexistence is possible — would strengthen the practical relevance.

**No empirical demonstration.** Because the paper is purely theoretical, there is no accompanying dataset or model audit showing *how severe* the fairness violations are in production LLMs. A companion empirical study would ground the theorem in lived harms.

## Further Reading

- **Kleinberg et al. (2016)** — "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores": the original fairness impossibility trilemma in classification.
- **Chouldechova (2017)** — "Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact": an independent proof of the same incompatibility, with focus on recidivism risk scores.
- **Bommasani et al. (2021)** — "On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models": the foundational survey that mapped fairness (and many other risks) onto the LLM landscape before impossibility proofs existed.
